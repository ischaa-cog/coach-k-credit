# How do I write a grant proposal?

**Straight answer.** A grant proposal is one argument made in sections: here is a real
problem, here is a workable plan, here are the people it helps, here is proof we can do it,
and here is what it costs. Coach K's core rule on the cheat sheet is exactly that — *be
specific about the problem, the plan, the people helped, and the proof that your plan can
work.*

**Before you write a word.** Read the full guidelines, including attachments, eligibility,
page limits, and formatting rules. Highlight the funder's priorities and use their language
accurately — not mechanically. Then write your one-sentence case:

> "We will [do what] for [whom] so that [measurable change]."

If you cannot finish that sentence, you are not ready to write the proposal yet.

**What reviewers need to believe.** Four things, and every section serves one of them:
- **A real need** — a short story plus evidence: who is affected, what is happening now, why
  action is needed.
- **A workable plan** — activities tied to a timeline, a responsible person, partners, and
  the resources required.
- **Measurable results** — what changes, how you count it, when you know you are on track.
- **Responsible stewardship** — a reasonable budget, stated assumptions, and what happens
  after the grant.

**The sections.** Follow the funder's requested order when it differs from this.

1. **Opening / executive summary** — the request, the need, the people served, the solution,
   the amount, in plain language. *"[Organization] requests $[amount] to [action] for
   [population] during [time period]."*
2. **Need or problem** — the gap, with local facts, credible sources, lived experience, and
   the consequences of inaction.
3. **Project design** — what happens, who does it, where, and why this approach fits. *"By
   month [x], we will [activity], reaching [number] [people] through [method]."*
4. **Goals and outcomes** — separate activities (what you do) from outcomes (what changes).
   Numbers, dates, and a measurement method.
5. **Team and partnerships** — relevant experience, roles, community trust, partners who make
   the plan stronger.
6. **Budget and justification** — match each major cost to an activity. Show your math: rate
   × quantity × time. Explain anything unusual, shared, or one-time.
7. **Sustainability and evaluation** — what you will learn, how you report progress, how the
   work continues.

**Coach K's REAL framework** for the narrative itself, from Funding Blueprint Live:
**R**elatable — share a personal connection or story. **E**ducate — explain the problem you
solve. **A**ction — outline how the funds will be used. **L**ovable — make your business
irresistible to funders.

**Budget sanity check.** Four questions: Is each cost *necessary* — could the project happen
without it? Is it *reasonable* — supported by a quote, prior cost, or market rate? Is it
*aligned* — do budget, timeline, activities, staffing, and the ask tell the same story? Is it
*complete* — supplies, travel, technology, accessibility, evaluation, indirect costs where
allowed? Do not pad "just in case", and never let totals conflict across forms.

**Red flags to strip out.**
- *Too broad*: "We will transform the community." Name the population, geography, activity,
  and measurable change.
- *Too vague*: "We will provide support." Say what support, how often, by whom, for how many.
- *Too perfect*: a credible plan names its assumptions and risks.
- *Too late*: a rushed submission misses an attachment or fails a formatting rule.

Replace "leverage", "impactful", and "innovative" with the concrete action or evidence those
words are standing in for.

**Use evidence like a professional.** Two or three strong facts beat a data dump. Name the
source and date for outside statistics. Pair a number with a human consequence. Say why the
evidence matters to *this* project.

**The reader test.** If someone unfamiliar with your work can summarize your need, plan, cost,
and expected result after one read, the proposal is doing its job.

**Final ten minutes before you submit.** Every eligibility rule met. Every required question
answered. The ask visible in the first paragraph. Numbers and dates agreeing everywhere. Each
outcome with a measurement plan. Budget matching the work plan. All attachments opening and
labeled. Someone else has proofread it.

**Close.** Members have the fill-in template — cover letter, letter of intent, executive
summary, need assessment, objectives, project plan, budget, sustainability. Start by writing
your one-sentence case at the top of a blank page, then let the template carry the structure.

Always follow the funder's official instructions where they differ from this. Educational
guidance, not a guarantee of funding.
