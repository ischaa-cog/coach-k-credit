2. Credit Utilization (30%)

This measures how much of your available credit you’re
using. It is calculated both per card and overall. The ideal
range is below 10%, while anything above 30% starts hurting
your score significantly—even if you’ve never missed a
payment.
For example, using $2,500 on a $5,000 limit means 50%
utilization, which can seriously lower your score.
Improvement strategies include paying balances before the
statement closing date (when reports are sent to bureaus),
requesting credit limit increases, and avoiding closing old
accounts since that reduces total available credit.


3. Length of Credit History (15%)

This factor looks at how long your accounts have been open,
including your oldest account, newest account, and average
account age. Longer credit history is always better.
A key rule here is never close old credit cards, even if unused,
because they help maintain your credit age. Keeping small
recurring charges on old accounts can keep them active and
beneficial.
You can also strengthen this factor by becoming an
authorized user on someone else’s older account.


4. Credit Mix (10%)

Lenders want to see that you can manage different types of
credit, not just credit cards. This includes:

   Revolving credit (credit cards)
   Installment loans (auto loans, student loans, mortgages)

A healthy mix shows financial responsibility and stability.
However, you should not take unnecessary debt just to
improve this category. Only add credit that makes sense for
your situation, such as a credit builder loan.
