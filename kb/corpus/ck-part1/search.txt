CHAPTER 2 FICO Deep Dive — Every Factor, Every Point This chapter breaks down how your FICO score is actually built and why it matters more than other scoring models like VantageScore when it comes to real financial decisions such as mortgages, auto loans, and business funding. While platforms like Credit Karma often show higher VantageScores, lenders typically rely on your true FICO score, which can be significantly lower. Tools like IdentityIQ can provide access to your real FICO scores from all three bureaus. FICO is based on five core factors, and understanding them gives you control over nearly every part of your credit profile. 1. Payment History (35%) This is the most important factor. Your score is heavily impacted by whether you pay on time. Even a single 30-day late payment can drop your score by 60–110 points. Negative items like collections and charge-offs are even more damaging, but their impact decreases over time with consistent positive behavior. The key insight is that recent payment behavior matters more than old mistakes. Building a consistent on-time payment pattern is the fastest way to recovery. Key actions include setting autopay, sending goodwill letters for late payments, and understanding that older negative items lose power over time—especially after two years of clean history. 2. Credit Utilization (30%) This measures how much of your available credit you’re using. It is calculated both per card and overall. The ideal range is below 10%, while anything above 30% starts hurting your score significantly—even if you’ve never missed a payment. For example, using $2,500 on a $5,000 limit means 50% utilization, which can seriously lower your score. Improvement strategies include paying balances before the statement closing date (when reports are sent to bureaus), requesting credit limit increases, and avoiding closing old accounts since that reduces total available credit. 3. Length of Credit History (15%) This factor looks at how long your accounts have been open, including your oldest account, newest account, and average account age. Longer credit history is always better. A key rule here is never close old credit cards, even if unused, because they help maintain your credit age. Keeping small recurring charges on old accounts can keep them active and beneficial. You can also strengthen this factor by becoming an authorized user on someone else’s older account. 4. Credit Mix (10%) Lenders want to see that you can manage different types of credit, not just credit cards. This includes: Revolving credit (credit cards) Installment loans (auto loans, student loans, mortgages) A healthy mix shows financial responsibility and stability. However, you should not take unnecessary debt just to improve this category. Only add credit that makes sense for your situation, such as a credit builder loan. 5. New Credit / Inquiries (10%) TEach hard inquiry (credit application) can temporarily lower your score by 5–10 points. Too many applications in a short period can make you look financially unstable. However, FICO allows rate shopping windows where multiple inquiries for mortgages or auto loans are grouped together if done within a short timeframe. Smart strategies include using pre-qualification (soft inquiries), spacing applications at least 6 months apart when possible, and understanding that hard inquiries fall off after 2 years. Qucik Win Strategy Two immediate actions can quickly improve your credit profile: Pay down any credit card above 30% utilization. Dispute any errors found on your credit report. These two steps alone can begin improving your score almost immediately. CHAPTER 4 Reading Your Credit Report Like a Pro Most people never read their credit report. That is like handing someone your bank card and never checking the statement. Errors on credit reports are MORE common than you think — studies show 1 in 5 consumers has a material error on at least one bureau. Every error that hurts you costs you money every single day until you fix it. Personal Information Section Name, address history, SSN, date of birth, and employment. Review carefully. Incorrect names or addresses can mean your file is mixed with someone else's — or indicate identity theft. Account Summary High-level overview of total accounts, balances, and payment status. Use this as your map before diving into individual accounts. Account History (Trade Lines) The core of your report. Each account: creditor name, account type, date opened, credit limit or loan amount, current balance, month-by-month payment history, and account status (open, closed, derogatory). This is where you find most of your disputes. Public Records Bankruptcies appear here. As of 2018, tax liens and civil judgments were removed from credit reports. If you see either of these, dispute immediately — they should NOT be there. Inquiries Hard inquiries (from applications) for the past 2 years. If you see an inquiry you did not authorize, dispute it using Template 2 in Chapter 10. Your Credit Report Review Checklist — Run This Every Time Accounts you don't recognize (could be fraud, identity theft, or a mixed file) Incorrect payment status (showing late when you paid on time) Duplicate accounts — the same debt listed twice Incorrect balances or credit limits Paid collections still showing a balance owed Discharged bankruptcy accounts still showing as open or owed Closed accounts showing as open Negative items older than 7 years (10 for Chapter 7 bankruptcy) Incorrect employer or address information Hard inquiries you did not authorize Any judgment or tax lien (these should have been removed in 2018) CHAPTER 5 Your Legal Rights Under the FCRA The Fair Credit Reporting Act is a federal law — your legal weapon. Congress passed it to protect consumers from inaccurate credit reporting. The credit bureaus and creditors are LEGALLY REQUIRED to honor these rights. When they don't — and they sometimes don't — they can be sued in federal court. Know your rights. Right to a Free Report One free report per bureau per 12 months at AnnualCreditReport.com. If you're denied credit, insurance, or employment due to your report, you get a free report within 60 days. Right to Dispute Inaccuracies Inaccurate, incomplete, or unverifiable information MUST be corrected or deleted within 30 days of your dispute — 45 days if triggered by your free annual report. Right to Know Who Pulled Your Credit You can see all hard inquiries on your report and know exactly who accessed your information and when. Right to Outdated Information Removal Most negatives must fall off after 7 years. Bankruptcy Chapter 7 falls off after 10 years. Chapter 13 after 7 years. Anything past its limit is ILLEGAL to report. Right to Direct Dispute with Creditors You can dispute directly with the CREDITOR (not just the bureau) when they're reporting inaccurate account-level information. This is called a direct dispute. Right to Sue Violations of the FCRA allow you to sue in federal court for actual damages, punitive damages, and attorney fees. Many consumer protection attorneys take these cases on contingency — meaning you pay nothing unless you win. The 'Unverifiable' Strategy — This Is How Entire Collections Get Removed: When you dispute an item, the bureau contacts the creditor and asks them to verify within 30 days. Many debt collectors — especially on older accounts — either ignore the request or cannot produce the original documentation. If they cannot verify, the item MUST be deleted. This is 100% legal. This is your right under federal law. Use it. Do you have a real website and professional business email? How long has your business been open? (even 30–90 days matters) What they are not usually focused on at this stage is your personal credit score. That’s the part most people get wrong. In some cases, they were approved for $10,000–$25,000 in net30 credit within 60 days, not because their personal credit was strong — but because their business looked legitimate. That’s the shift. Credit Cards, Charge Cards CHAPTER 12 & Revolving Accounts: Know the difference Most people use the words credit card and charge card like they mean the same thing. They do not — and the difference matters a lot for your credit score, cash flow, and funding strategy. Let me break all three down so you never confuse them again. Credit Cards (Revolving Credit) A credit card is a revolving credit account. That means you have a set credit limit, you can charge up to that limit, carry a balance from month to month, and make minimum payments. The key word is REVOLVING — the credit replenishes as you pay it down. This is the most common type of credit product in America. With a charge card: You MUST pay the full balance every month No option to carry a balance No APR on purchases Usually no preset spending limit Spending adjusts based on usage and income The most well-known charge cards: American Express Green American Express Gold American Express Platinum American Express Centurion (Black Card) These are CHARGE cards — not credit cards. Because charge cards do not report a traditional credit limit, they typically do NOT affect utilization the same way — which can actually HELP your score. Because they don't report traditional limits: ✔ They don't inflate utilization ✔ They strengthen payment history ✔ They improve profile strength ⚠ But miss the full payment and consequences are severe. Charge cards do not allow partial payments. Know the product before you apply. Revolving Accounts — The Broader Category Revolving credit is the CATEGORY that includes credit cards, lines of credit, and home equity lines of credit (HELOCs). What makes them revolving is the cycle: you borrow, you repay, the credit is available again. This is different from installment accounts (car loans, mortgages, student loans) which have a fixed repayment schedule with a defined end date. ACCOUNT AFFECTS CATEGORY EXAMPLES TYPE UTILIZATION? Hard Pulls vs. Soft Pulls: CHAPTER 13 What Hits Your Score and What Doesn't I cannot tell you how many people I've met who were afraid to check their own credit — terrified they'd hurt their score. Let me put that fear to rest right now. Checking your own credit NEVER hurts your score. That is a soft pull. What hurts your score is applying for new credit — that is a hard pull. Here is exactly how they work: Hard Inquiries (Hard Pulls) — These Affect Your Score A hard inquiry occurs when a lender or creditor pulls your credit report to make a lending decision because you applied for credit. Hard inquiries are what the bureaus call "a sign of new risk." The logic is simple: If you're applying for a lot of credit at once, you might be in financial trouble. Each hard pull typically drops your score by 5–10 points temporarily. Hard Pulls Are Generated By: • Applying for a credit card (personal or business) • Applying for a car loan or auto financing • Applying for a personal loan or debt consolidation loan • Applying for a mortgage or refinance • Applying for a student loan (private loans) • Some apartment rental applications (varies by landlord) • Some utility company applications in new service areas • Business credit applications requiring a personal guarantee Hard inquiries stay on your report for 2 years but only affect your score for 12 months. This means you can apply with: 5 mortgage lenders, 4 auto lenders, and multiple student loan lenders...and your score only sees ONE inquiry.
