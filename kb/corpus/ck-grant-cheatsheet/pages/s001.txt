GRANTFIND — GRANT PROPOSAL CHEAT SHEET
Practical Funding Guide. Make your proposal easy to fund.

Use this guide to turn a good idea into a clear, credible request that answers the funder's
questions and makes the next step obvious.

THE CORE RULE
Be specific about the problem, the plan, the people helped, and the proof that your plan
can work.

SECTION 1 — BEFORE YOU WRITE

DO THIS FIRST
- Read the full guidelines, including attachments, eligibility, page limits, and formatting rules.
- Highlight the funder's priorities and repeat their language accurately, not mechanically.
- Make a one-sentence case: "We will [do what] for [whom] so that [measurable change]."
- Build a deadline plan with time for documents, review, signatures, and submission.

AVOID THIS
- Starting with a generic template before understanding the funder.
- Applying when you do not meet a basic eligibility requirement.
- Waiting until the deadline to request letters, quotes, or financial records.
- Trying to solve every problem in one proposal.

SECTION 2 — WHAT REVIEWERS NEED TO BELIEVE

Show a real need: use a short story plus evidence -- who is affected, what is happening now,
and why action is needed.
Show a workable plan: connect activities to a timeline, responsible person, partners, and
the resources required.
Show measurable results: define what will change, how you will count it, and when you will
know you are on track.
Show responsible stewardship: make the budget reasonable, explain assumptions, and show how
the work continues after the grant.

READER TEST — If someone unfamiliar with your work can summarize your need, plan, cost, and
expected result after one read, your proposal is doing its job.

SECTION 3 — A STRONG PROPOSAL, SECTION BY SECTION
Give every paragraph a job. A clear structure helps reviewers find the answers they are
scoring. Follow the funder's requested order when it differs.

1. Opening / executive summary — state the request, the need, the people served, the
solution, and the amount in plain language.
   "[Organization] requests $[amount] to [action] for [population] during [time period]."
2. Need or problem — describe the gap with local facts, credible sources, lived experience,
and the consequences of inaction.
3. Project design — explain what will happen, who will do it, where it will happen, and why
this approach fits the need.
   "By month [x], we will [activity], reaching [number] [people] through [method]."
4. Goals and outcomes — separate activities (what you do) from outcomes (what changes). Use
numbers, dates, and a measurement method.
5. Team and partnerships — show relevant experience, roles, community trust, and any
partners who make the plan stronger.
6. Budget and justification — match each major cost to an activity. Explain unusual, shared,
or one-time expenses.
7. Sustainability and evaluation — describe what you will learn, how you will report
progress, and how the work continues or scales.
